Course Makeover: Listening and Asking Better Questions

I took Lesson 1 of FEMA’s public Effective Communication course (IS-242.b), a classroom lesson built around instructor-led slides and activities, and rebuilt it as a 15-minute self-paced course in Articulate Rise. Here are two of the changes side by side.

What the original does

The lesson opens with logistics: sign-in sheets, evaluation forms, breaks, restrooms, cell phones. None of it helps anyone listen better, and the only visual is a clock.

What I changed

  • Opened with a short scene: a call between two coworkers that goes wrong.
  • Cut the room logistics, which don’t apply to a self-paced course.
  • Rewrote the objectives as five things the learner will practice.

Why

A recognizable problem gives the learner a reason to keep going. By the end of the scene they have seen what poor listening costs before anyone has taught them a thing.

From a table of cues to a picture you can explore

How the lesson teaches nonverbal signals.

AfterLesson 5, built in Rise

Each marker opens a note. Select the image to see it full size.

What the original does

A slide defines “clusters” over a photo of someone yawning. The manual adds a table of cues, and says body language is more than 90 percent of a message and words only 7 percent.

What I changed

  • Replaced the lists with one picture and four signals to explore: words, tone, face and posture.
  • Each note says what a signal could mean and where it stops being reliable.
  • Added a decision: he says “Fine” in a flat voice while looking away. What does the learner do next?

Why

I dropped the 90/7 statistic. It is usually traced to small studies of how people judge feelings when words and tone conflict, and it doesn’t describe communication in general. What holds up is the lesson’s own point: one signal proves little, and when signals disagree, check instead of assuming.

See how the rest of it works

The full course has a branching scenario with three decisions, two sorting activities, and knowledge checks that ask the learner to choose a response, not recall a definition.
